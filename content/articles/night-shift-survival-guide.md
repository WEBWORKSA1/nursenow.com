Night shift asks your body to be alert when its internal clock expects sleep, and to sleep when the world is bright and loud. You can’t fully fix that biology, but you can work with it. These strategies come from sleep and shift-work research and from what experienced night nurses actually do.

[[toc]]

## Protect your main sleep

- **Make your bedroom a cave:** blackout curtains or an eye mask, cool temperature, white noise or earplugs, phone on do-not-disturb.
- **Go to sleep soon after you get home.** The longer you stay up, the more your body’s morning alerting signal fights you.
- **Aim for a consistent sleep window** on work days — even 6–7 protected hours beats broken naps.
- **Tell your household your schedule** and put a sign on the door if needed.

## Use light like a tool

Light is the strongest signal to your body clock.

- **Bright light early in your shift** helps you stay alert.
- **Dim, warm light toward the end** of the shift helps you wind down.
- **Sunglasses on the drive home** reduce the morning light that tells your brain it’s time to wake up.

## Time your caffeine

Caffeine helps alertness, but it lingers — its half-life averages around five hours and varies widely between people. Front-load caffeine in the first half of your shift and avoid it in roughly the last six hours before you plan to sleep.

## Strategic naps

- A **short nap before your first night** (or late afternoon before each shift) can reduce sleepiness.
- If your workplace allows protected breaks, a **20–30 minute nap** can improve alertness without deep-sleep grogginess. Give yourself a few minutes to wake fully before patient care.

## Eating on nights

Your digestion slows overnight. Many night nurses feel better with a **substantial meal before the shift**, lighter snacks overnight (protein, fruit, nuts, yoghurt), plenty of water, and a light snack before sleep rather than a heavy meal. Bring food from home so you’re not relying on vending machines at 4 a.m.

## Schedule smarter

- **Cluster your nights** instead of scattering them across the week.
- **Avoid quick returns** — finishing a night and starting a day shift with little rest. Our [shift planner](/tools/shift-planner/) flags these automatically.
- After a run of nights, take a **short recovery sleep** the morning you finish, then get up and stay active in daylight to reset for days off.

## Drowsy driving is the hidden risk

The drive home after a night shift is one of the riskiest parts of the job. If you’re nodding off: pull over safely and nap, use a ride or transit, or rest before leaving. Warning signs include drifting between lanes, missing exits and heavy eyelids.

## When to talk to a clinician

If you have persistent insomnia, extreme sleepiness on and off shift, or you rely on sleep aids, talk to a healthcare provider. **Shift work disorder** is a recognised condition, and treatment — including safe use of any sleep aid — should be guided by a clinician, not a coworker. Pregnancy, chronic conditions and medications also change what’s safe for you.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many hours of sleep do night shift nurses need?
Adults generally need at least seven hours per 24-hour period. On nights, that may come from a main sleep plus a short nap. Prioritise a protected main sleep.

### Should I stay up or sleep after my last night shift?
Many nurses take a short recovery sleep of a few hours that morning, then stay up in daylight and go to bed at a normal time to reset for days off.

### Is it safe to take melatonin for night shifts?
Ask a healthcare provider before using melatonin or any sleep aid; timing, dose and suitability depend on your health and other medications.
