Canada needs nurses, and internationally educated nurses (IENs) are a big part of how provinces fill shifts. But the path has two separate tracks — **professional registration** and **immigration** — and many people lose months by doing steps out of order. Here is the sequence that works.

[[toc]]

## Two tracks, running in parallel

1. **Registration** with the nursing regulator in the province where you want to work. This decides whether you can practise as an RN, RPN/LPN or NP.
2. **Immigration** — a work permit or permanent residence. This decides whether you can live and work in Canada.

Neither guarantees the other. A job offer doesn’t license you, and a nursing licence doesn’t give you the right to work in Canada. Plan both from the start.

## Step 1: Choose your province and nursing category

Nursing is regulated provincially, and rules differ. Decide where you want to live and which designation fits your education:

- **Registered nurse (RN)** — typically a bachelor’s-level nursing education.
- **Licensed/registered practical nurse (LPN; RPN in Ontario)** — diploma-level.
- **Nurse practitioner (NP)** — graduate-level, with a separate process.

Your choice affects which exam you write and which gaps you may need to close.

## Step 2: Apply to NNAS (outside Québec)

For most IENs applying to a regulator outside Québec, the first stop is the **National Nursing Assessment Service (NNAS)**. NNAS:

- verifies your identity, nursing education, registration history and work experience directly with the source institutions;
- compares your education to Canadian standards; and
- sends an **advisory report** to the regulator(s) you choose.

NNAS does **not** license you and doesn’t decide your eligibility. Expect document requests to your school, previous regulators and employers — start early, because institutions abroad can be slow. Check the current fee schedule on nnas.ca before you apply.

## Step 3: Apply to the provincial regulator

Once NNAS has sent your report, apply directly to the regulator — for example the College of Nurses of Ontario (CNO) or the British Columbia College of Nurses and Midwives (BCCNM). The regulator reviews your file and tells you what’s required next. Common outcomes:

- you’re eligible to write the registration exam;
- you need a **competency assessment** (an evaluation of your knowledge and skills); or
- you need **additional education** such as a bridging program or specific courses.

Some provinces have created faster pathways for nurses from certain jurisdictions or with recent practice — check your regulator’s current rules rather than relying on forums.

## Step 4: Language proficiency

If your nursing education wasn’t in English (or French, where applicable), expect to prove language proficiency with an approved test such as **IELTS Academic** or **CELBAN**. Each regulator publishes its accepted tests and minimum scores, and results usually have an expiry date — time your test so it’s valid when the regulator reviews your file.

## Step 5: Bridging and competency gaps

Bridging programs, offered by colleges and universities, help IENs close specific gaps — Canadian health-system context, pharmacology, clinical practice — often with clinical placements. They can take one or two semesters. Ask your regulator exactly which outcomes you must meet so you choose the right program.

## Step 6: Write the registration exam

- **RNs** (outside Québec) write the **NCLEX-RN**, the same exam used in the United States.
- **Practical nurses** write the **REx-PN** or **CPNRE**, depending on the province.
- Many regulators also require a **jurisprudence** exam on provincial law and professional standards.

Start NCLEX practice early — even while you wait for documents. Our [60-day NCLEX plan](/blog/how-to-pass-the-nclex-first-try/) and the [daily question](/nclex/) are free.

## Québec is different

Québec does not use NNAS for nurse registration. The **Ordre des infirmières et infirmiers du Québec (OIIQ)** evaluates IEN equivalence directly, may require an integration or training program, and administers its own professional exam. You must also demonstrate French proficiency to practise in Québec. If Québec is your goal, start with the OIIQ and French-language preparation.

## The immigration track

Common routes include employer-supported work permits and permanent residence through federal programs such as **Express Entry** — which has held category-based draws for healthcare occupations — and **Provincial Nominee Programs**, several of which prioritise nurses. Being registered (or well along the registration process) can strengthen an application. Immigration rules change frequently; use official Government of Canada sources or a licensed immigration consultant (RCIC) or lawyer, and be wary of anyone guaranteeing a job or visa for a fee.

## A realistic timeline

Every file is different, but many IENs spend somewhere between several months and two years moving from NNAS application to registration, depending on document speed, assessment outcomes and whether bridging is required. Working in a related role — such as a personal support worker or health care aide — while you finish can build Canadian experience and income.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need NNAS if I want to work in Québec?
No. Québec’s regulator, the OIIQ, runs its own equivalence process, and French proficiency is required to practise there.

### Can I write the NCLEX before I arrive in Canada?
That depends on your regulator. The regulator must first determine you are eligible to write. NCLEX test centres exist in many countries, so some IENs write abroad once approved.

### Does a job offer mean I’m licensed?
No. Only the provincial regulator can register you to practise. Employers may offer conditional roles or support while you complete registration.

### How long does NNAS take?
It depends mostly on how quickly your school, regulators and employers send documents. Many applicants report several months; start requesting documents as early as possible.
