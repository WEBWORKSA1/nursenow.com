If you’ve ever looked at a travel contract or a telehealth job and wondered whether you need a license in every state, the Nurse Licensure Compact (NLC) is the answer for much of the country. Here’s how it actually works.

[[toc]]

## What the compact is

The NLC is an agreement between US states and territories that lets a registered nurse or licensed practical/vocational nurse hold **one multistate license** issued by their home state and practise — in person or by telehealth — in every other compact state.

It began in 2000 and was replaced by the **Enhanced NLC** in 2018, which added uniform licensure requirements such as fingerprint-based criminal background checks. As of this writing, **more than 40 US jurisdictions** have enacted it. A few large states, including California and New York, have not joined, and some states have enacted the law but are still implementing it. Always check the live map on [nursecompact.com](https://www.nursecompact.com/) before you make plans.

## Who qualifies for a multistate license

You apply through the board of nursing in your **primary state of residence (PSOR)** — the state where you legally live (think driver’s license, voter registration, tax filing). To receive a multistate license you must meet uniform licensure requirements, which include:

- graduating from an approved nursing education program (or an equivalent for IENs);
- passing the NCLEX-RN or NCLEX-PN;
- holding an active, unencumbered license;
- submitting fingerprints for a state and federal criminal background check; and
- meeting other conduct-related standards.

If your PSOR is **not** a compact state, you can’t get a multistate license — you’d hold single-state licenses and apply by endorsement elsewhere.

## Practising across states

With a multistate license, you can practise in any other compact state under a **privilege to practise**. Key rule: you must follow the **nurse practice act of the state where the patient is located**, not your home state. That matters for scope of practice, delegation rules and documentation.

## When you move

- **Moving to another compact state permanently:** you must apply for a license in your new home state. The commonly cited rule is to apply within **60 days** of establishing residency; you can keep practising on your former multistate license while that application is processed.
- **Moving from a compact state to a non-compact state:** your multistate license converts to a single-state license, and you’ll need a license in your new state.
- **Temporary assignments (like travel contracts):** your PSOR doesn’t change just because you take a 13-week contract elsewhere.

## Telehealth and remote nursing

Licensure follows the patient. A telehealth nurse calling patients across many states needs the right to practise in each of those states — which is why employers in remote nursing often prefer, or require, a compact license. ## Advanced practice: the APRN Compact

Nurse practitioners and other APRNs are **not** covered by the NLC. A separate **APRN Compact** has been adopted by some states, but it only takes effect once a minimum number of states enact it. Until then, NPs need a license in each state where they practise.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is my compact license valid in California or New York?
No — not unless those states join the compact. You would need a California or New York license. Check the live list on nursecompact.com because membership changes.

### Can I hold a multistate license if I live in a non-compact state?
No. Eligibility depends on your primary state of residence being a compact state.

### Does the compact cover LPNs and LVNs?
Yes. The NLC covers both RNs and LPN/VNs. It does not cover APRNs.

### Do I need to tell anyone when I work in another compact state?
You don’t need a separate license, but you must follow that state’s practice laws, and your employer will verify your multistate status.
