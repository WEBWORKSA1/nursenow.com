Nursing Interview Questions and Answers: 20 Questions With STAR Examples

The nursing interview questions you’ll actually be asked — behavioural, clinical and situational — with STAR answer frameworks, example answers and the questions you should ask the unit.

By NurseNow Editorial TeamUpdated November 10, 20263 min read

Nursing interviews follow a pattern: a few questions about you, several behavioural questions (“tell me about a time…”), some clinical scenarios (“what would you do if…”), and your chance to ask questions. Prepare for the pattern and you’ll walk in calm.

The STAR method

Behavioural answers land best when they follow STAR:

  • Situation: one or two sentences of context (no patient identifiers).
  • Task: what you were responsible for.
  • Action: what you did — the longest part.
  • Result: the outcome, ideally measurable, plus what you learned.

Aim for 60–90 seconds per answer. Prepare six to eight stories you can adapt: a deteriorating patient, a conflict, a mistake, a difficult family, teamwork under pressure, advocacy, time management and a time you went above and beyond.

About you

1. Tell me about yourself. Present → past → future in under a minute: your current role, the experience that prepared you for this one, why this unit.

2. Why do you want to work here? Name something specific: the unit’s patient population, a residency program, Magnet status, a value or initiative.

3. What are your strengths and an area for growth? Choose a real growth area and show what you’re doing about it.

Behavioural questions (use STAR)

4. Tell me about a time a patient’s condition changed suddenly. Example: “On a night shift, a post-op patient became newly confused with a heart rate of 128 and BP of 92/50 (S). I was his primary nurse (T). I completed a focused assessment, checked his glucose and oxygen saturation, raised the head of the bed, called a rapid response and gave SBAR to the team (A). He was treated for early sepsis and transferred to step-down; the surgeon credited the early call (R).”

5. Describe a conflict with a coworker and how you resolved it. Focus on private, respectful conversation and the patient-safety outcome.

6. Tell me about a mistake you made. Choose a real, resolved example, show that you reported it per policy, and describe the system change or habit that followed.

7. Describe a time you advocated for a patient.

8. Tell me about a time you managed a heavy assignment. Show prioritisation, delegation and asking for help early.

9. Describe a difficult family interaction. Show empathy, clear communication and escalation when needed.

10. Tell me about a time you taught a patient something complex. Mention teach-back.

Clinical and situational questions

11. Two call lights and a rapid response at once — what do you do? Prioritise by acuity (ABCs, unstable before stable), delegate appropriately, communicate.

12. A physician gives an order you believe is unsafe. Clarify, use your chain of command, don’t carry out an unsafe order, document.

13. You suspect a colleague is impaired. Patient safety first; follow policy and escalate to your manager.

14. A patient refuses medication. Explore why, educate, respect autonomy, notify the prescriber and document.

15. How do you prevent falls / infections / medication errors? Name concrete practices: hourly rounding, bed alarms, hand hygiene, the rights of medication administration, scanning.

Questions for new grads

16. How do you handle stress? Show healthy, specific strategies.

17. Where do you see yourself in five years? Show commitment to growing on this unit — a certification, precepting, charge.

18. What did you learn in your preceptorship?

Closing questions

19. Why should we hire you? Three matches between your strengths and their needs, in under 45 seconds.

20. Do you have any questions for us? Always yes.

Questions to ask the unit

  • What are typical nurse-to-patient ratios on days and nights?
  • How long is orientation, and how is it adjusted for new grads?
  • What’s the float policy?
  • How are schedules made, and how often is self-scheduling possible?
  • What support exists for certification and continuing education?
  • What do nurses who thrive here have in common?

Compare offers with the pay calculator before you decide — ratios and orientation can matter more than an extra dollar an hour.

Frequently asked questions

What should I wear to a nursing interview?

Business casual or professional attire is safest, even for bedside roles. Some employers invite candidates to interview in scrubs during hiring events — follow their instructions.

How do I answer “tell me about a time you made a mistake”?

Choose a real, resolved example; describe how you identified and reported it, what you did to keep the patient safe, and what you changed afterwards.

Should I send a thank-you note?

Yes. A short email within 24 hours that references something specific from the conversation is a simple way to stand out.
