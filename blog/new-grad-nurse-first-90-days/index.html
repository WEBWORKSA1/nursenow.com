New Grad Nurse Survival Guide: Your First 90 Days

A practical plan for your first 90 days as a new graduate nurse — orientation, working with your preceptor, a brain sheet, time management, safe medication habits, SBAR and protecting your wellbeing.

By NurseNow Editorial TeamUpdated November 10, 20263 min read

The first three months as a nurse are some of the hardest of your career — and some of the fastest learning you’ll ever do. Feeling overwhelmed is normal. These habits help you get through orientation safely and come out confident.

  2. Build a brain sheet

Days 1–30: learn the system

  • Know your orientation plan. Ask your preceptor and educator for the checklist, the competencies you’ll be signed off on, and how long orientation lasts.
  • Learn where things live: crash cart, rapid response process, policies, supply rooms, who to call for what.
  • Shadow first, then share. Start by taking part of your preceptor’s assignment and build up gradually.
  • Write down every question and ask at a calm moment — unless it’s about safety, then ask immediately.

Build a brain sheet

A brain sheet is a one-page tracker for each patient: diagnosis, code status, allergies, vitals, assessments, lines and drains, medication times, labs, and to-dos by hour. Many experienced nurses still use one. Develop a template you like and keep patient information secure — shred it at the end of the shift.

Days 31–60: build speed safely

  • Cluster care: combine assessment, medications and comfort rounds when you enter a room.
  • Prioritise with ABCs and stability, not just the order of your task list.
  • Chart as you go rather than saving it all for the end.
  • Use SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) whenever you call a provider. Have vitals, recent labs and the medication list in front of you before you dial.

Medication safety habits

  • Check the rights of medication administration every time and scan every dose.
  • Look up any medication you don’t know before you give it.
  • Use independent double checks for high-alert medications (insulin, heparin, opioids, potassium) according to policy.
  • Speak up when an order doesn’t make sense — clarifying is part of your job.

Days 61–90: own your assignment

  • Take a full assignment with your preceptor as backup.
  • Practise delegation: what can be safely handed to assistive staff, and how to follow up.
  • Ask for feedback weekly — what’s going well, and one thing to work on.
  • Learn the escalation path for when a patient “just doesn’t look right”. Trust that instinct and act on it.

Protect yourself

  • Sleep and food are not optional. Plan meals and rest around your rotation with the shift planner.
  • Expect a dip. Many new grads hit a low point a few months in. It passes; reach out to peers, your educator or your EAP.
  • Find your people: a new-grad cohort, a mentor, a friend on the unit.
  • Keep your credentials current: track BLS and other expiries in the licence tracker.

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal to feel incompetent as a new grad nurse?

Yes. Most new graduates feel this way for the first months. Competence grows with repetition, feedback and time; feeling unsure means you’re taking safety seriously.

What should I do if orientation feels too short?

Talk to your educator or manager early with specific examples of what you still need. Many units can extend orientation.

When should I start thinking about a specialty certification?

Once you meet the practice-hour requirement — often after one to two years in a specialty. See certifications that raise your pay.
